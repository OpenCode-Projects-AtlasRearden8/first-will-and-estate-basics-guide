# Powers of Attorney: Who Acts If You Can't

A will speaks for you after you die. Powers of attorney speak for you **while you're
alive but can't act** — illness, injury, surgery recovery, a trip where you're
unreachable, or the slow kind of change that comes with age.

Most planning guides treat this as chapter 8 material. It's chapter 4 here because,
frankly, **an incapacitation plan is more likely to be used in your actual life**
than any other page in your folder.

## The core idea in one breath

A power of attorney (**POA**) is a document that says: *"I authorize this named person
(the agent, or attorney-in-fact) to do these specific things on my behalf."*

You stay you. Your agent isn't taking over your life — they're **allowed to act in
certain ways when you can't**, like a deputy with a defined badge, not a replacement.

## The two main kinds

| Kind | Covers | Typical moment it's used |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Financial / general POA** (also called a durable general power of attorney) | Money and property: talk to banks, pay bills, manage accounts, sign documents, handle taxes, sell property if authorized | Hospital stays, cognitive decline, extended travel, deployment |
| **Medical POA** (healthcare proxy / agent) — covered fully in chapter 5 | Medical decisions when you can't communicate | Surgery complications, critical care, end-of-life conversations |

Some states use paired documents; some use one combined "advance directive" that
includes the medical POA. **Your state's forms and naming vary — a professional or
your state's official forms are the reliable source.**

## "Durable": the word that matters

A **durable** power of attorney **survives your incapacitation**. A non-durable one
ends exactly when you'd need it most, which is a little like an umbrella built to
fold in the rain. When a form asks durable versus springing, durable is what makes
the document useful.

**Springing vs. immediate:** some states allow a POA that takes effect only when a
doctor certifies incapacity ("springing"). Others require it to be effective on signing, with trust in your agent as the safeguard. Both are legitimate designs;
professionals have opinions about which fits which family.

## Why everyone should have one (even the invincible)

Picture month one of a hospitalization, without a POA:

- Nobody can freely discuss your accounts with the bank — privacy laws (from HIPAA
  to bank policy) lock your info even from spouses and adult children.
- Bills float. Autopay lapses on a closed card. Insurance deadlines pass.
- To act fully, family may need a court-appointed **conservatorship/guardianship** —
  an expensive, slow, public process that makes strangers of a judge and your family's
  calendar.

With a POA in the folder: your agent makes one phone call with the document in hand
and keeps the household running. That's the entire difference. People fight
conservatorship battles in the news because **no paper existed quietly
announcing the obvious**.

## Choosing your agent

The job is: **organized enough, trusted completely, and hard to steer.**

- Financial agent: someone detail-adjacent — they'll keep receipts and calendars.
  Spouse? Common and fine. Adult child as backup? Common. Business partner? Only
  with careful limits.
- **Name at least one backup.** Agents get sick, move abroad, predecease.
- Consider splitting roles: one person for money, another for healthcare. That's
  normal, and often wise.
- Tell them they've been named, and where the papers are. A surprised agent is a
  stranded agent.

Boundaries and checks are healthy, not distrustful: a formal (non-durable) POA for
a specific real-estate closing, or instructions in your letter limiting credit
lines — professionals can structure documents to fit the trust level you actually
have. And if you'd want a second set of eyes on money movement, say so in your
letter of instruction: "Please have a second family member review any transfers over $X."

## Limits, revocation, and updates

- You can make the POA **broad** (everything) or **narrow** (one account, one closing).
- You can **revoke** in writing anytime you have capacity — tell your agent, tell
  your bank, destroy copies you distributed.
- **Banks are choosy.** Some accept any valid POA; some want their own form or a
  recent signed original (they fear stale or forged documents). Practical tip: once
  executed, present it to your key institutions *while nothing's wrong* — far easier
  to file a POA on a Tuesday than during a crisis.
- Update after big moves between states — POA effectiveness and form requirements
  vary by state; professionals re-paper cross-state plans.

## What POAs don't do

- They don't override beneficiary designations or transfer assets at death (nothing
  to do with your will's distribution job).
- A POA generally **ends at your death** — then the will/estate process takes over.
- They don't work for things that genuinely require *you*: voting, marriage, your
  passport, most sworn statements.

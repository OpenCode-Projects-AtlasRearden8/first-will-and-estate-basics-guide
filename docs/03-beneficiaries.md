# Beneficiaries: Where the Money Actually Goes

Here's the plot twist of this whole guide, compressed into one sentence: **most of
a person's money doesn't care what their will says.** Retirement accounts, life
insurance, and bank accounts with payable-on-death forms pass by *beneficiary
designation* — the little form you signed when you opened the account. That form
outranks the will.

If you change nothing else after reading this guide, do this chapter.

## What a beneficiary designation is

When you opened your 401(k), IRA, life insurance policy, or set up certain bank
accounts, a question was asked: *"Who should receive this if you die?"* Your answer
was recorded on the account itself. When the account owner dies, the account
**bypasses the will and probate entirely** and goes straight to the named person(s).

The account types built this way:

- **401(k)s, 403(b)s, pensions** — name a beneficiary and usually a contingent one
- **IRAs and similar accounts** — beneficiary set with the custodian
- **Life insurance** — the beneficiary *is* the entire mechanism
- **Payable-on-death (POD) bank accounts** and **transfer-on-death (TOD)** brokerage
  accounts or vehicle/deed registrations, where your state offers them
- **Health savings accounts (HSAs)** — spousal rules are particular; professionals love these questions

> Verify locally: TOD availability for vehicles, deeds, and other registrations
> varies by state. The general principle — a named beneficiary passes outside the
> will — does not.

## The golden rule

**Designations beat wills. Every time. No exceptions you want to live in.**

Real-world shape of this rule: the will was updated last year to say "everything to
my sister." The 401(k) form — signed in 2016 — says "my former partner." The 401(k)
form wins. This exact scenario is probably the most common avoidable estate-planning
disaster in existence, and it costs one login to fix.

## A 60-minute beneficiary audit

Grab coffee and a list of your accounts. For each one:

1. **Find** the beneficiary page (usually under account settings → beneficiaries).
2. **Look** at the primary and contingent names.
3. **Ask**: is this who I would choose today, knowing I get no re-do?
4. **Update** anything stale — life changes (marriage, divorce, birth, death), employer
   changes (old 401(k)s collect dust and old names), and decade-old defaults ("estate"
   or blank) are the usual culprits.
5. **Screenshot or save** the confirmation. (Keep it with your storage map, chapter 8 —
   but never store account numbers or sensitive details in any file you sync or
   version-control.)

Employer-sponsored plans sometimes require calling HR or the plan administrator to
update. Annoying, yes. Do it anyway.

## Primary, contingent, and the trap of leaving it blank

- **Primary beneficiary** — first in line.
- **Contingent beneficiary** — backup if the primary has predeceased you or can't
  be located. Name one. A missing contingent sends the account *back into your
  estate* to be handled by the will and probate after all — the exact detour you
  were trying to avoid.
- **Blank beneficiary** — the dreaded default. The account may pay to your estate
  or follow the custodian's default rules. Neither is a plan.

Leaving a designator as "Estate of…" is often the weakest option: it drags the money
into probate and can expose it to creditors. (When an outright-person beneficiary
isn't workable — e.g., a minor child or a special situation — professionals have
better structures for it; that's a real attorney question.)

## Minors, trusts, and "I want to give it, carefully"

Another spot where a professional earns their fee: **a minor child cannot simply
inherit and manage money.** If you name your child directly, a court-managed
arrangement (often a **custodianship**) may be needed, with its own rules and
timelines. If you want money to arrive *graded* — at certain ages, for support,
for education — that's what **trusts** are for. The vocabulary is in the glossary;
the design work is squarely a "hire a pro" zone (chapter 9).

## Percentage thinking beats dollar thinking

For IRA/401(k) designations, express shares in **percentages** (60% / 40%), not
dollar amounts or "my half goes to…" phrases. Accounts change size; percentages
don't care. And when naming multiple primaries, read the form's fine print once —
custodians differ on edge cases like what happens if one beneficiary has died,
which is exactly the kind of clause worth knowing you agreed to.

## The pet clause (yes, really)

Pets are *property* in legal terms almost everywhere — which sounds cold and means
a will can leave them (and money for their care) to a person, or set up a small
**pet trust** in many states. Your letter of instruction (chapter 6) is the
companion piece: vet's name, food, meds, quirks, "she hates the carrier." Whoever
inherits the pet will treasure that page.

# Your Will: What It Does and What It Can't

A will is the document most people mean when they say "I should get my affairs in
order." This chapter explains what it actually is, in plain terms, and — just as
important — what it does **not** do, because the gaps are where people get surprised.

## What a will actually is

One sentence: **a will is your written instruction for what happens to your stuff when
you die — and who is in charge of getting it there.**

The two jobs inside that sentence:

1. **Distribution** — who receives what (property, savings, keepsakes, the record collection).
2. **Administration** — naming an **executor** (sometimes called a *personal
   representative*) — the trusted person who shepherds your estate through the
   legal process, with the court's supervision.

If you have minor children, a will is also where you **nominate a guardian** — the
person you'd want to raise them. That alone makes a will worth having for any parent.

## What a will does NOT do

This is the part worth reading twice:

| A will does **not** control… | What controls it instead |
| --- | --- |
| Retirement accounts, life insurance, payable-on-death (POD) / transfer-on-death (TOD) accounts or deeds | Their **beneficiary designations** (chapter 3) |
| Jointly owned property with right of survivorship | The ownership title automatically |
| Anything held in a trust | The trust document |
| Decisions while you're **alive but incapacitated** | Powers of attorney & directives (chapters 4–5) |
| Pet rules in most places | Varies; a pet trust or letter instructions help |
| Funeral preferences, in practical effect | Your **letter of instruction** (chapter 6) — curiously, funerals often happen before a will is even read |

A will is one instrument in a small band, not a soloist. The good news: each
instrument is simple, and chapter 7's checklist covers all of them.

## Intestacy: what happens with no will

If you die without a will (called dying **"intestate"**), your state has a default
plan for you — a legal formula that splits property by family relationship rules.
Notes on that formula:

- It often gets *close* to what you'd have wanted. **Close** is the operating word.
- It can't pick an executor you trust over someone court-appointed by priority rules.
- It can't name a guardian for your children — that's decided by a judge, from scratch.
- Unusual family situations, unmarried partners, and stepchildren are where the
  default formula tends to miss worst.

So the honest framing is: **no will = letting the state write your will by algorithm.**
Most people, given the choice, would rather write it themselves.

## The cast of characters

- **Testator** — you, the will-writer (a will "testifies" to your wishes; the word is
  old-fashioned but you'll see it on forms).
- **Executor** — the doer. Pick someone organized, responsible, and — this matters —
  *likely to outlive the paperwork process with their sense of humor intact*. Name a
  **backup executor** too.
- **Guardian** — for minor children. This is a heart decision, not a suitability
  contest; pick the person whose values and household feel right, and name a backup.
- **Witnesses** — most states require one or two adults (often not beneficiaries)
  to watch you sign. Some states allow a fully "self-proving" notarized affidavit.
  **Valid signing rules vary by state — this is exactly the kind of detail a
  professional confirms for your location.**

## How people actually get one

Three routes, in rough order of cost and effort:

1. **Attorney-drafted** — best for anything nonstandard: prior marriages, children
   from different relationships, business ownership, significant or complicated
   assets, special-needs considerations, property in multiple states, or if you just
   want certainty. Chapter 9 covers choosing one.
2. **Reputable online will service** — reasonable for a straightforward estate:
   one marriage, uncomplicated assets, standard wishes. Choose a well-established
   service, follow its state-specific guidance, and execute the signing exactly per
   your state's rules.
3. **Statutory or handwritten wills in some states** — a few states recognize
   do-it-yourself or handwritten forms under strict conditions. Only go this route with confirmed,
   state-specific information — this is a "verify locally" item, not a guess item.

A middle pattern many people use: draft simple documents online now, then have an
attorney review them in a one-hour consult. Cheaper than starting from scratch,
safer than going it completely alone.

## Common myths, quickly retired

- *"Everything automatically goes to my spouse."* Usually mostly true, but not
  universally — single biggest shared-asset surprises involve accounts where the
  named beneficiary is an ex. Which is why chapter 3 exists.
- *"I'm too young/poor for a will."* Guardianship nominations alone make a will
  essential for young parents; and even a modest estate is painful to unwind intestate.
- *"A will helps me avoid probate."* Mostly the opposite — **probate is the court
  process that validates your will.** It can be slow and it can cost money; avoiding
  or simplifying it (where that's even desirable) is a separate strategy — beneficiary
  designations, small-estate procedures, sometimes trusts — but it's a topic for a
  professional, not a DIY checkbox.
- *"Once it's done, it's done."* It's version 1.0. Plan to revisit after big life
  events or every 3–5 years.

# Healthcare Directives: Your Voice When You Can't Speak

This is the chapter people put off the longest, and it's the one their families
are most grateful for later — because it does two jobs:

1. Names **who** speaks for you medically (a healthcare proxy/agent).
2. Records **what** you want said (your treatment wishes).

Neither of these is a doom document. Frame it the way clinicians actually use it:
this is **pre-op paperwork for your whole life** — the same energy as a surgery
consent form, filled out while you're calm and nobody's nervous.

## Why this document matters so much

Without a directive, medical decisions fall to whoever state law says (usually next
of kin in a defined order). They'll *try*, they'll love you — but they'll be guessing
about your wishes by strangers in a place with fluorescent lights and time pressure. That guessing is
the part families describe as the hardest part of the hardest week.

With a directive, the conversation shifts from *"What would they have wanted?"*
(a philosophical exam) to *"They told us exactly what they wanted"* (a checklist).

## The pieces

An "advance directive" is a catch-all phrase. Depending on your state it bundles one
or both of:

- **Healthcare (medical) power of attorney / appointment of a healthcare proxy** —
  names your **agent** to make medical decisions when you can't. This agent can be
  **different from your financial agent**; that's normal and sometimes wise.
- **Living will** — your instructions about specific situations that tend to come up:
  life-sustaining treatment (resuscitation, ventilation, feeding tubes), pain
  management priorities, and organ/tissue donation preferences.

Many states publish **free statutory forms** — a fill-in-the-blank advance directive
that hospitals and providers immediately recognize. Search "[your state] advance
directive form" and prefer results from the official state health department, state
bar, or a hospital system. (In some states a POLST/MOST-style form — a doctor-signed
medical order for people with serious illness — also exists; that's a
clinical conversation, not paperwork you draft alone.)

## Choosing your medical agent

The medical agent's job description, honestly stated:

- Can be **present** (or immediately reachable) during crises.
- Can **talk to doctors** without shrinking: asking questions, hearing hard news,
  repeating themselves until clear.
- Knows your values, not just your vitals: what "quality of life" means to you
  specifically.
- Can bear the room's weight. (Not everyone can. That's fine. Choose who can.)

Name a **backup**. Then set them up to succeed — an agent with zero context is
guessing too. Which brings us to…

## Talking about your wishes (the 20-minute version)

You don't need a solemn family summit. You need ~one conversation, maybe split over
two dinners, using the directive as the prop. Conversation prompts that work:

- *"I filled out one of those healthcare directive forms. Want to know what I picked?"*
- *"If something ever went really wrong, the thing I'd want you to fight for is ___."*
- *"I'd want you to argue for tests before decisions" / "I'd want you to lean toward
  comfort — do you understand which one I mean?"*
- *"You don't have to guess. It's written down. I just want to know you'd follow it."*

Clinicians recommend these conversations precisely because **a written form plus a
family who heard it once** is twice as effective as a form alone.

## What goes in the living will

Prompts to think through (your state's form will phrase these formally):

- Resuscitation (CPR) — yes/no depending on situation.
- Mechanical ventilation, dialysis, tube feeding — for how long, under what conditions.
- Pain control priority — full comfort measures even if they also hasten things.
- Organ and tissue donation — and registering your preference through your state's
  donor registry.
- Mental health treatment preferences, where your state's forms allow them.
- A values paragraph in your own words: what you're afraid of, what "enough" means.
  Doctors and agents read this paragraph more than you'd think.

You may change or revoke any of it while you have capacity — this document is a
standing instruction, not a contract against your future self.

## Practical logistics (the part nobody tells you)

- **Give copies out.** Agents, backups, your doctor's office, and one in your
  storage folder (chapter 8). A directive in a drawer at home during a vacation
  emergency helps no one; hospitals ask for the document.
- Some people carry a card or phone wallpaper noting *"Healthcare directive copies
  with [agent name, phone]"* — consider what your state/hospital system offers for
  digital registration.
- Re-execute per your state's rules when you update (witnesses/notary requirements
  vary — treat state rules as authoritative).

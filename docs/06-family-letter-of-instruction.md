# The Family Letter of Instruction

If the will is the legal document, the **letter of instruction** is the human one.
It's informal (handwritten, typed, emailed, or even a voice memo is fine), it isn't
a court filing, and it's often **the single most-read page** in the whole folder.

Think of it as *the README for your household*, written for people having a
difficult week.

## What goes in it

There's no official form — which is the point. Plain language, your voice, and the
practical stuff no other document carries:

**People to call, roughly in order**
- Your executor, agents, backups — with phone numbers.
- Your attorney, accountant, insurance agent, financial advisor, if you have them.
- Your employer and HR contact.

**Where things are**
- The storage map summary (chapter 8): where the will, POAs, directives, deed,
  titles, and tax files live.
- Any safe-deposit box location + who's a signer, plus where the **key** lives
  (this detail wins prizes).

**Household logistics**
- Bills on autopay and which card they run on, roughly.
- Pets: names, vets, meds, food, quirks — *"the dog refuses dinner without the
  Sinatra playlist; do not judge us both."*
- House quirks: where the water shutoff is, which neighbor has a spare key, the
  Netflix password, the thermostat's opinion.

**Work and accounts (at the pointer level, not the password level)**
- Employer name; noting that the login details live in your password manager with
  who's the authorized recovery contact (the *digital-legacy-handbook* covers this
  in depth; here you just point to it).

**Wishes that aren't legal**
- Funeral/memorial preferences — burial or cremation, which kind of service,
  music you'd want, who should (and shouldn't) be asked to speak.
- Charities for memorial donations.
- Bits of grace: who to call first, what you'd want said to your kids, one joke
  that always lands.

**A warm paragraph.** Superpower move: last page, handwritten, *"I planned all of
this on an ordinary Tuesday, which is proof I'm not thinking about endings on
purpose — I just couldn't stand the idea of this being hard for you."* That page
gets kept for decades.

## Style guidelines that work

- **Bullets, not essays.** This document will be read under stress. Bullet points
  are kindness.
- **Pointers, not contents.** Your letter should say *where* account statements
  are — never write full account numbers or passwords in a loose letter. Use your
  password manager's emergency-access feature or sealed, separate envelope; the
  letter should reference those systems rather than contain them.
- **Date it and re-date it.** People trust a stale letter less than no letter.
  A "last updated" line tells readers the map is current.
- **It's revisable.** Keep the master somewhere findable and treat edits like
  software releases: v1 now, patch when life changes.

## Antipatterns to avoid

- Turning it into a legal will (it isn't one, it can't replace one, and courts may
  disregard it — keep legal wishes in legal documents).
- Storing it only on your locked computer that nobody else has access to.
- Including full passwords or account numbers in plain text — a gift to
  identity thieves, not your family.
- Waiting for it to be perfect. A bullet list on notebook paper > a beautiful
  letter in a drawer that doesn't exist.
